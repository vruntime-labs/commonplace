# Commonplace Editorial Policy

**Status:** Approved v0.1  
**Applies to:** Commonplace-authored, community-contributed, and externally referenced content

## 1. Purpose

Commonplace exists to make useful developer knowledge easier to discover, understand, evaluate, and share. This policy sets the standards for content published in Discover and Learn, including original work written by the Commonplace editorial team.

The goal is trustworthy, practical knowledge, not the largest possible catalogue. Publication should help readers make informed decisions and build better software.

## 2. Content origins must be clear

Commonplace publishes and curates several distinct kinds of material:

- **Commonplace editorial:** original guides, tutorials, explainers, engineering articles, references, checklists, and learning tracks written or maintained by the Commonplace editorial team.
- **Community-contributed:** resources, guides, corrections, discoveries, and practical experiences submitted by contributors.
- **External resources:** third-party projects and materials described, evaluated, or linked from Commonplace.
- **Maintainer-curated collections:** public collections and learning tracks assembled by maintainers from items with their own origins.

The page or record must identify its author or source and its editorial provenance. Curating or linking to a third-party item is not the same as creating it, endorsing it, or independently verifying it.

Commonplace-authored content is not automatically independently verified. Editorial ownership identifies responsibility for the content, not a guarantee of correctness.

## 3. Editorial principles

Published content should be:

1. **Accurate:** factual and technical claims should be supported by appropriate evidence.
2. **Useful:** explain a real concept, decision, tool, workflow, or problem.
3. **Clear:** use plain language, meaningful structure, and specific terminology.
4. **Accessible:** welcome beginners without removing technical depth for experienced readers.
5. **Practical:** include relevant examples, prerequisites, limitations, and next steps.
6. **Tool- and model-aware:** explain dependencies and trade-offs without unjustified preference or lock-in.
7. **Honest:** distinguish facts, opinions, estimates, assumptions, and unverified claims.
8. **Maintainable:** identify version-sensitive material and revisit it when circumstances change.

Avoid hype, unsupported superlatives, fake benchmarks, fabricated experience, misleading comparisons, and promotional copy disguised as neutral guidance.

## 4. Research and sources

Authors and reviewers should prefer sources in this order, according to the claim being made:

1. Official documentation, specifications, standards, release notes, and primary project repositories.
2. Original research, technical papers, reproducible experiments, and first-hand evidence.
3. Maintained technical publications and reputable specialist sources.
4. Community discussions and personal experience as supplementary context.

Community discussions can reveal real-world problems and practical trade-offs, but a comment or popular opinion is not by itself proof of a technical claim.

Citations should be placed near the claims they support. Sources must be relevant to the statement, accessible where possible, and accurately represented. If reliable evidence is unavailable, narrow the claim or state the uncertainty rather than inventing certainty.

## 5. Original Commonplace publishing

The Commonplace editorial team may publish original, in-depth technical tutorials, guides, explainers, engineering articles, references, checklists, and learning tracks.

Editorial owners are responsible for:

- Defining the intended audience, outcome, and prerequisites.
- Researching claims and linking reliable sources.
- Testing important instructions and examples where practical.
- Stating when examples have not been tested.
- Recording version assumptions, limitations, and risks.
- Reviewing content when a material change makes it inaccurate or misleading.
- Correcting errors and maintaining attribution.

Original content should add explanation, synthesis, practical context, or a useful learning path. It must not be copied from third-party articles or documentation without permission and proper attribution. Paraphrasing does not remove the need to cite source ideas or facts.

## 6. Community contributions

Community contributions are welcome from beginners and experienced developers. Contributions may include new entries, guides, tutorials, articles, corrections, source updates, and practical experience.

Reviewers should preserve the contributor's intended meaning and credit. Editing for clarity, structure, accessibility, accuracy, or consistency is permitted within the project's contribution terms. Substantial changes should be discussed with the contributor where practical.

A contribution is not published automatically. Reviewers may request changes, defer it, or decline it with a concise explanation.

## 7. External resources and recommendations

When describing a third-party resource:

- Identify the project, publisher, maintainer, and canonical source where possible.
- Explain its purpose and intended audience without overstating capabilities.
- Record relevant licence, version, compatibility, setup, and maintenance information when known.
- Disclose material permissions, data access, network access, or security considerations.
- Separate observed facts from reviewer opinion.
- State unknowns honestly.

Inclusion in Commonplace does not mean that the project is officially endorsed, sponsored, audited, or guaranteed to be safe. Do not use labels such as “verified” or “secure” unless the scope, evidence, and date of the check are clear.

## 8. Technical examples and testing

Examples should be minimal enough to understand and complete enough to use safely. State the relevant language, tool, runtime, version, operating system, or environment when these affect the result.

Where practical, run commands and code examples in a representative environment before publication. Record meaningful test context. If an example has not been tested, say so. Never invent test results, performance measurements, benchmarks, or compatibility claims.

Commands that modify files, change permissions, expose data, incur costs, or affect production systems must include appropriate context and warnings. Prefer safe defaults and explain destructive steps before presenting them.

## 9. Security-sensitive content

Commonplace is an educational developer knowledge platform. Content must support legitimate learning, development, administration, and defensive security.

Reviewers should assess whether instructions could expose credentials, compromise systems, enable unauthorised access, or cause destructive effects. Do not publish secrets, private data, exploit instructions intended to facilitate harm, or instructions that knowingly compromise users or systems.

For legitimate security education, include scope, authorisation assumptions, safe test environments, and defensive context. Do not execute submitted code, prompts, skills, or MCP servers as part of editorial review. Flag suspicious material for maintainer and security review.

Editorial acceptance is not a security audit or a guarantee that referenced software is safe.

## 10. Attribution, originality, and licensing

Contributors must have the right to submit the work and must identify third-party material, authors, sources, and applicable licences. Reviewers should check attribution and reuse permissions before publication.

Do not assume that content found online is free to copy, modify, or redistribute. Do not remove licence notices or imply that Commonplace owns third-party work.

The platform's source-code licence, editorial-content licence, contributor agreement, and detailed reuse terms remain open project decisions. This policy does not silently select a licence or grant Commonplace rights that have not been agreed. Those terms must be resolved and communicated before the relevant content is accepted under them.

## 11. Editorial review and review transparency

Review should consider the dimensions relevant to the item:

- Relevance and usefulness.
- Accuracy and supporting sources.
- Clarity, structure, and accessibility.
- Attribution, originality, and licensing.
- Metadata completeness.
- Version and compatibility claims.
- Safety and security considerations.
- Maintenance ownership and freshness.

Record what was actually checked. A content review, source check, licence check, compatibility check, and security review are different activities. Completing one does not imply completion of the others.

The review process is not a guarantee of correctness, legal compliance, or security. Readers should be able to distinguish unknown information from information that has been checked.

## 12. Corrections, disputes, and withdrawal

Commonplace should correct material errors promptly when they are identified. Corrections may be proposed through GitHub or the guided website contribution route when that route becomes available.

For disputed claims, reviewers should examine the evidence, seek clarification where useful, and explain material decisions. Where a claim cannot be substantiated, qualify or remove it.

Content may be marked as needing an update, archived, or withdrawn when it is obsolete, misleading, unsafe, improperly attributed, or no longer suitable for publication. Where appropriate, retain a brief explanation and preserve a record of material editorial changes.

Security reports should follow the repository's security disclosure process rather than public issue discussion of exploitable details.

## 13. Collections and learning tracks

Collections and learning tracks should have a clear purpose, audience, and inclusion criteria. Maintainers are responsible for keeping their sequence and links useful. Each included item must retain its own author, source, provenance, and review information.

Curation is not endorsement. Do not include items merely to inflate catalogue size or imply that a sequence is comprehensive when it is not.

## 14. Accessibility and inclusive language

Use descriptive headings, meaningful link text, readable paragraphs, accessible tables, and text alternatives for meaningful visuals. Avoid relying on colour alone to communicate status. Explain specialist terminology when it first matters, and do not assume a reader's experience, location, device, or background.

Examples and learning material should be respectful and avoid discriminatory, harassing, or needlessly exclusionary language. Follow the project's Code of Conduct.

## 15. Conflicts of interest and commercial relationships

Authors and reviewers should disclose material affiliations, sponsorships, employment, or other interests that could reasonably affect a recommendation or comparison. Do not present paid placement or personal preference as independent evaluation.

Commonplace should not fabricate rankings, ratings, adoption numbers, testimonials, or popularity signals. Any future commercial relationship or sponsorship policy must be explicit and visible to readers.

## 16. Maintenance responsibilities

Every maintained item should have a clear owner or responsible team where possible. Review priority should be based on risk and likelihood of change, with particular attention to security guidance, installation instructions, version-sensitive resources, and rapidly changing tools.

When an owner can no longer maintain an item, maintainers should reassign responsibility, mark it as needing an update, archive it, or withdraw it. Do not leave outdated content presented as freshly verified.

## 17. Policy maintenance

This policy is maintained as part of the Commonplace repository. Changes to editorial standards should be proposed transparently and reviewed like other governance documentation. The policy should evolve as the community and publishing process mature, without weakening the principles of attribution, accuracy, safety, and honest provenance.
